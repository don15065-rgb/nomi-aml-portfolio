Banking Court

Sued by a bank: defending a recovery suit in Karachi

A Banking Court suit runs on a thirty day clock and its own rules. This guide explains what the summons means, what must be filed, and what can still be done after a decree.

By Noman Sohail, Advocate High Court, Karachi. Last reviewed November 2026.

If a bank has sued you, or you have just seen your name in a newspaper notice from a Banking Court, the single most important thing to understand is that this is not an ordinary civil suit. The Financial Institutions (Recovery of Finances) Ordinance 2001 gives banks a fast, special procedure. A defendant who treats it like a normal case usually discovers the difference after the decree.

The thirty day rule

In a banking suit you have no automatic right to defend. You must apply for leave to defend within thirty days of first service of the summons. If you do not, the bank’s allegations are treated as admitted and the court may decree the suit straight away.

How a banking suit differs from an ordinary suit

What a leave to defend application must contain

This is where most defences are won or lost. The application is not a letter of protest. The Ordinance requires it to be in the form of a written statement and to state specifically:

  1. the amount of finance you actually availed, with dates;
  2. the amounts you have paid to the bank, with dates of payment;
  3. the amount of finance and other sums you accept as payable, up to the date of the suit; and
  4. the amounts you dispute, and the facts supporting that dispute.

An application that skips these particulars is liable to be rejected on that ground alone. Leave is granted where the application raises a substantial question of law or fact that needs evidence to decide. A general denial does not meet that standard. Figures and documents do.

What I examine in the bank’s claim

No two files are the same, and none of the following is a formula. These are the areas where bank claims most often turn out weaker than the plaint suggests:

  • The statement of account. The plaint must be supported by a statement certified under the Bankers’ Books Evidence Act 1891. Incomplete, uncertified or internally inconsistent statements are common.
  • Mark-up. Whether mark-up was charged beyond the agreed rate or after the facility period ended, and whether mark-up has been charged on mark-up.
  • Payments. Deposits and instalments not credited, or credited late.
  • The documents. Finance agreements signed in blank, facilities renewed without fresh documents, and guarantees stretched to cover finance they were never given for.
  • Limitation. Whether the suit was filed within time from the default the bank itself relies on.
  • Disbursement. Whether the finance claimed was in fact disbursed to you in the manner pleaded.

If a decree has already been passed

Many people first learn of a banking case when their account is attached or a court officer arrives. The options narrow at this stage, and each has its own clock:

  • Setting aside the decree. Where the summons was not duly served, an application can be made within twenty-one days of the decree or of your first knowledge of it.
  • Appeal. To a bench of the High Court of Sindh within thirty days of the decree.
  • Objections in execution. The bank can attach and sell property, attach bank accounts and salary, and seek arrest and detention. Each mode has legal limits, and property that does not belong to the judgment debtor cannot be taken.
  • Settlement. A decree does not end the possibility of settlement. Banks frequently accept structured or lump sum settlements at the execution stage.

If your CNIC has been blocked in execution, that is a separate grievance with its own remedy. The superior courts have recently held against blocking identity cards to enforce money decrees.

Credit cards and personal loans

Credit card and personal loan recoveries go to the same Banking Court under the same Ordinance, however small the sum. The thirty day rule applies in full. Because the amounts are modest, cardholders tend to ignore the summons, and a decree for a figure swollen by charges follows unopposed. These are also the cases where the statement of account most rewards a careful reading.

If you have a claim against the bank

The Ordinance works in both directions. A customer can sue a financial institution in the Banking Court for breach of its obligations under a finance. For service failures, unauthorised debits and similar retail grievances, the Banking Mohtasib offers a faster route that does not carry court fees, provided the matter is not already before a court.

What to bring to a first consultation

  • The summons, plaint and every annexure, exactly as received, with the envelope if it shows a date
  • The newspaper notice, if that is how you found out
  • Your sanction or offer letter and the finance agreements
  • Your own record of payments: receipts, deposit slips, bank statements
  • Any letters, emails or settlement offers exchanged with the bank
  • For guarantors and mortgagors: the guarantee and the property documents

Common questions

Short answers to what people ask first. Every case turns on its own documents, so treat these as orientation, not advice on your matter.

I received a Banking Court summons. How long do I have?

Thirty days from the date you were first served by any one of the modes the law allows: a bailiff, registered post, courier or newspaper publication. Within that time you must file an application for leave to defend. The time runs from the earliest of those modes, not from the day the papers actually reached your hands, so count conservatively.

I missed the thirty days. Is the case finished?

Not necessarily, but the position is serious and you should act immediately. The court can condone delay only for sufficient cause, and Banking Courts apply this strictly. If a decree has already been passed and the summons was never properly served, an application to set the decree aside can be filed within twenty-one days of learning of it. An appeal to the High Court lies within thirty days of the decree.

Can I be arrested because of a bank loan or credit card debt?

Failing to repay a loan is a civil liability, not in itself a crime. However, once a decree is passed, arrest and detention in civil prison is one of the modes of execution a court may order, subject to conditions designed to protect a debtor who genuinely cannot pay. Separately, a dishonoured cheque or the dishonest removal of charged assets can give rise to criminal proceedings. The two tracks should not be confused.

The bank claims far more than I borrowed. Is that allowed?

A bank may claim the outstanding principal, the contractual mark-up, and cost of funds from the date of default. It may not claim whatever figure it chooses. The plaint must be supported by a properly certified statement of account, and the entries in it are open to challenge: mark-up charged after the facility expired, payments not credited, and charges with no basis in the agreement are the usual points of examination.

Will the bank settle?

Often, yes. Banks regularly settle recovery suits, particularly for a lump sum, and a settlement can be recorded by the court as a compromise decree. Two cautions: do not let settlement talks cause you to miss the thirty day deadline, and do not pay anything under an arrangement that is not recorded in writing and, where a suit is pending, placed before the court.

My CNIC has been blocked in a bank case. Is that lawful?

Executing courts in Karachi have in the past directed NADRA to block the CNICs of judgment debtors. The superior courts have recently ruled against that practice as a means of enforcing a money decree. A blocked card can be challenged, first before the executing court itself and, where necessary, before the High Court of Sindh in its constitutional jurisdiction.

I am only the guarantor. Can the bank proceed against me?

Yes. A guarantor's liability is ordinarily co-extensive with that of the principal borrower, and banks routinely sue borrower, guarantors and mortgagors together. A guarantor does, however, have defences of his own: the terms and limit of the guarantee, whether the facility was renewed or altered without his consent, and whether the claim against him is within time.
