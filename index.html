The Pakistani court hierarchy

Every case travels a route. This map sets out the courts, tribunals and forums of Pakistan with a Sindh focus: who hears what, under which statute, and where the appeal goes next. Select a pathway to trace a matter from first instance to the Supreme Court, or expand any forum for the detail.

Tier I · Apex Court

Supreme Court of Pakistan

Constitution of Pakistan, 1973 · Arts. 176–191

  • Original jurisdiction in matters of public importance involving fundamental rights (Art. 184(3)).
  • Appellate jurisdiction over High Courts: appeals as of right and by leave (CPLA) under Art. 185.
  • Advisory jurisdiction on a reference by the President (Art. 186); review of its own judgments (Art. 188).
  • Decisions bind all other courts in Pakistan (Art. 189). Branch Registry at Karachi.
  • Also hears appeals from Service Tribunals (Art. 212(3)) and, through its Shariat Appellate Bench, from the Federal Shariat Court.

Appeal lies to: Final court · review under Art. 188 only

Tier II · Constitutional Courts

High Court of Sindh

Constitution, Arts. 175, 192–203 · CPC 1908 · CrPC 1898

  • Writ jurisdiction under Art. 199: certiorari, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and habeas corpus against public authorities.
  • Original civil jurisdiction at Karachi for suits above the prescribed value (currently PKR 15 million — verify before filing).
  • Appellate side: first and second appeals and revisions (ss. 96, 100, 115 CPC); criminal appeals and confirmation of death sentences (ss. 410, 374 CrPC).
  • Statutory appellate forum for Banking Courts (s. 22 FIO 2001), tax references (s. 133 ITO 2001), ATA and accountability appeals; company and admiralty jurisdiction.
  • Principal seat at Karachi with benches and circuit courts at Hyderabad, Sukkur, Larkana and Mirpurkhas.

Appeal lies to: Supreme Court (Art. 185); Intra-court appeal (LRO 1972, s. 3) from original side

Federal Shariat Court

Constitution, Arts. 203A–203J

  • Examines whether laws are repugnant to the Injunctions of Islam (Art. 203D) and may strike them down prospectively.
  • Appellate and revisional jurisdiction in cases under the Hudood laws.
  • Suo motu power to examine any law within its remit.

Appeal lies to: Shariat Appellate Bench, Supreme Court (Art. 203F)

Tier III · District Judiciary (Sindh)

District & Sessions Judge

Sindh Civil Courts Ordinance 1962 · CrPC 1898

  • Principal court of original civil jurisdiction in the district; administrative head of the district judiciary.
  • Civil side: first appeals against decrees of Civil Judges within the prescribed pecuniary limit; rent appeals (s. 21, SRPO 1979); family appeals (s. 14, Family Courts Act 1964).
  • Sessions side: trials of offences punishable with death or life imprisonment; appeals against convictions by Magistrates (s. 408 CrPC); bail and criminal revisions.
  • Additional District & Sessions Judges exercise the same powers on cases made over to them, including designated Gender-Based Violence and consumer courts.

Appeal lies to: High Court of Sindh (appeal / revision)

Senior Civil Judge

Sindh Civil Courts Ordinance 1962

  • Distributes and supervises civil business among Civil Judges in the district.
  • Exercises an enhanced pecuniary class of civil jurisdiction.
  • Deals with succession and guardianship-related civil work assigned under the relevant statutes.

Appeal lies to: District Judge or High Court, by value

Civil Judge & Judicial Magistrate

Sindh Civil Courts Ordinance 1962 · ss. 30, 32 CrPC

  • The workhorse of the system: tries civil suits within its pecuniary class (Class I, II or III).
  • Wears a second hat as Judicial Magistrate: criminal trials with sentencing powers graded by class (a Magistrate First Class may pass sentences up to three years).
  • Grants interim injunctions, appoints commissions and conducts execution proceedings in suits before it.

Appeal lies to: Civil decree → District Judge or High Court, by value; Criminal conviction → Sessions Court (s. 408 CrPC)

Family Court

Family Courts Act 1964 (as amended in Sindh)

  • Exclusive jurisdiction over dissolution of marriage, dower, dowry, maintenance, custody and guardianship, and jactitation.
  • Summary, comparatively informal procedure designed for speed; strict evidence rules relaxed.
  • Decrees for dissolution on the ground of khula are not appealable.

Appeal lies to: District Judge (s. 14) · thereafter Art. 199 in appropriate cases

Rent Controller

Sindh Rented Premises Ordinance 1979

  • Eviction of tenants (s. 15) on grounds including default, personal bona fide need and reconstruction.
  • Fixation of fair rent and orders for deposit of rent during proceedings.
  • A creature of statute: jurisdiction confined strictly to the Ordinance.

Appeal lies to: First Rent Appeal → District Judge (s. 21); Then Art. 199, High Court

Tier IV · Special Courts

Banking Court

Financial Institutions (Recovery of Finances) Ordinance 2001

  • Exclusive jurisdiction over suits for recovery of finances between customers and financial institutions (s. 9).
  • The leave-to-defend regime (s. 10): the defendant must obtain leave, disclosing a substantial question, before contesting; default invites an immediate decree.
  • Interim decrees, sale of mortgaged property and execution with the powers of a civil court; criminal jurisdiction over offences under the Ordinance.
  • A core forum of my practice and research interest in banking litigation.

Appeal lies to: High Court, Division Bench (s. 22) — within 30 days

Anti-Terrorism Court

Anti-Terrorism Act 1997

  • Trial of scheduled offences under the Act with statutory timelines aimed at expedition.
  • Special regimes for remand, protection of witnesses and transfer of cases.
  • Constituted and supervised in consultation with the High Court.

Appeal lies to: High Court (s. 25) — within 15 days

Accountability Court

National Accountability Ordinance 1999

  • Trial of references involving corruption, corrupt practices and assets beyond known sources of income.
  • Distinct arrest, bail and plea-bargain framework under the Ordinance, as repeatedly reshaped by amendment.

Appeal lies to: High Court (s. 32) — within 10 days

Consumer Protection Court

Sindh Consumer Protection Act 2014

  • Claims for damages arising from defective products and faulty services.
  • Presided over by judges of District Judge rank designated under the Act; simplified claim procedure.

Appeal lies to: High Court of Sindh

Labour Court

Sindh Industrial Relations Act 2013

  • Individual grievance petitions, unfair labour practices and industrial disputes within the province.
  • Trans-provincial establishments fall to the National Industrial Relations Commission instead.

Appeal lies to: Sindh Labour Appellate Tribunal; NIRC for trans-provincial matters

Environmental Protection Tribunal

Sindh Environmental Protection Act 2014

  • Contraventions of environmental standards, EIA conditions and protection orders within Sindh.
  • Mixed membership of judicial and technical members.

Appeal lies to: High Court of Sindh

Tier V · Tribunals, Revenue & Quasi-Judicial Forums

Sindh Service Tribunal

Sindh Service Tribunals Act 1973 · Art. 212

  • Exclusive jurisdiction over the terms and conditions of service of provincial civil servants, after departmental remedies are exhausted.
  • The jurisdiction of ordinary courts, including the High Court, is constitutionally barred in such matters (Art. 212).

Appeal lies to: Supreme Court directly, by leave (Art. 212(3))

Appellate Tribunal Inland Revenue

Income Tax Ordinance 2001 · Sales Tax Act 1990

  • The apex fact-finding forum in the tax chain: assessment by an Officer of Inland Revenue, appeal to the Commissioner (Appeals), then to the Tribunal.
  • Recent amendments have re-routed appeals by pecuniary value between the Commissioner (Appeals) and the Tribunal — verify the current thresholds.
  • Findings of fact end here; only questions of law travel upward.

Appeal lies to: Reference to High Court on a question of law (s. 133 ITO 2001); Then Supreme Court

Board of Revenue, Sindh

Sindh Land Revenue Act 1967

  • The revenue hierarchy: Mukhtiarkar → Assistant / Deputy Commissioner → Commissioner → Member, Board of Revenue, through appeal, review and revision (ss. 161–164).
  • Mutations, record-of-rights and partition disputes live here; questions of title belong to the civil courts.
  • Revenue orders are amenable to the writ jurisdiction once departmental remedies are exhausted.

Appeal lies to: Art. 199, High Court of Sindh (writ)

Ombudsman Institutions

Wafaqi Mohtasib Order 1983 · BCO 1962, Part IVA · FTO Ordinance 2000

  • Wafaqi Mohtasib and Provincial Mohtasib (Sindh) for maladministration by government agencies; Federal Tax Ombudsman for tax maladministration.
  • Banking Mohtasib for complaints against banks: a fast, fee-free alternative to litigation for retail banking grievances.
  • Not courts, but an increasingly important first stop before, or instead of, formal proceedings.

Appeal lies to: Representation to the President / Governor against findings

Appellate pathways

Civil suit

  1. Civil Judge (trial)
  2. District Judge (first appeal)
  3. High Court (second appeal / revision)
  4. Supreme Court (CPLA)

An ordinary-value civil suit. Where the value exceeds the District Court’s appellate limit, the first appeal goes straight to the High Court; the highest-value suits at Karachi begin on the High Court’s original side.

Criminal trial

  1. Judicial Magistrate (trial)
  2. Sessions Court (appeal, s. 408 CrPC)
  3. High Court (appeal / revision)
  4. Supreme Court

For offences triable by a Magistrate. Serious offences are tried by the Sessions Court at first instance, with appeal to the High Court (and confirmation of death sentences under s. 374 CrPC).

Family matter

  1. Family Court (decree)
  2. District Judge (appeal, s. 14)
  3. High Court (Art. 199, where available)

Khula decrees are non-appealable; constitutional petitions lie only within the narrow limits the case law allows.

Banking recovery

  1. Banking Court (suit + leave to defend)
  2. High Court, Division Bench (s. 22, 30 days)
  3. Supreme Court (CPLA)

The FIO 2001 route: a recovery suit, the leave-to-defend gate at s. 10, then a single statutory appeal to a Division Bench of the High Court.

Tax appeal

  1. Commissioner (Appeals)
  2. Appellate Tribunal IR
  3. High Court (reference, s. 133)
  4. Supreme Court

Facts are settled at the Tribunal; only questions of law reach the High Court by reference, and onward to the Supreme Court.

Constitutional petition

  1. High Court, Art. 199 (writ)
  2. Supreme Court, Art. 185 (appeal)

Judicial review of executive or administrative action where no adequate alternate remedy exists. Matters of public importance involving fundamental rights may also be taken directly to the Supreme Court under Art. 184(3).

Pecuniary limits and appeal periods are stated as a practitioner’s working guide and change by amendment. Verify the current figures before filing.

Research behind the practice

Alongside litigation, my academic work focuses on banking law, financial regulation, capital markets and regulatory design, and on how legal rules shape institutional behaviour.

Financial regulation

The Secondary Competitiveness and Growth Objective in UK Financial Regulation

Whether the post-FSMA 2023 competitiveness mandate risks encouraging deregulatory drift, examined through the regulatory behaviour of the PRA and the FCA. LLM dissertation, University of Edinburgh.

Banking law

Banking Regulation After the Global Financial Crisis

Prudential regulation, supervisory objectives and the Basel standards, and the post-crisis balance between financial stability, institutional resilience, competitiveness and growth.

Capital markets

Misleading Financial Information and Capital Markets

Private and public law mechanisms addressing false or misleading information in financial markets, including issuer liability, disclosure standards and the limits of enforcement.

Structured finance

Securitisation and Financial Alchemy

The benefits, risks and regulatory challenges of securitisation: special purpose vehicles, tranching, liquidity creation, systemic-risk concerns and the post-crisis reforms.

This background is not decoration. It is what lets me read document-heavy disputes, banking matters and regulatory problems with more depth than the pleadings alone require.

About

I am an Advocate enrolled with the Sindh Bar Council, practising as a civil and criminal lawyer in Karachi from an office in Clifton. My work runs from the trial courts to the appellate forums: pleadings, interim relief, evidence and appeal, in property, contract and tenancy matters, and bail, complaints and trial defence on the criminal side.

Before returning to practice in Pakistan, I read for an LLM in International Banking Law and Finance at the University of Edinburgh, graduating with Distinction, after an LLB from the University of London. That background shapes how I litigate: financial disputes are rarely just about the money claimed, they are about the regulatory architecture sitting behind the transaction.

Alongside practice, I hold the CAMS certification in anti-money laundering and maintain an active research interest in financial regulation, public law and the structure of the Pakistani court system, some of which you can see mapped on this page.

I take the unfashionable view that a lawyer’s first duty to a client is clarity: about the law, about the realistic outcomes, and about the cost of getting there.

What clients can expect

Direct communication, careful review of documents, honest assessment of risks, and legal work prepared with seriousness.

No false drama. No unnecessary complexity. No promises that the law itself does not support.

Reduced fees in cases of genuine hardship

Good representation costs money, and not everyone who needs it can afford the full fee. Where a client is in genuine financial difficulty, I am willing to agree a reduced professional fee, decided case by case.

A reduced fee is not free representation: I do not take matters free of charge. The preparation, the candour about prospects and the standard of advocacy are the same as on any other brief.
